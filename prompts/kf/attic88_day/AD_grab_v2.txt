A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. An attic window in the Netherlands, a winter morning in 1988, seen from behind and above the right shoulder of a five-year-old Chinese girl who kneels at the low sill. The back of her head is close to us and large at the left of the picture. Her right arm, in a white jacket sleeve, reaches up from the bottom of the picture to the glass in the middle, where her small hand still points with its index finger at a frost fern on the pane. From the RIGHT edge of the picture a grown woman's bare forearm comes straight in, nearly level, and her hand has just closed around the child's forearm between wrist and elbow. Only the woman's forearm and hand are in the picture, with the rolled edge of a cream blouse sleeve at the right edge: no more of her. Behind the hands: the lower right pane of an old four-pane window, its vertical glazing bar just right of the child's head, the wooden side of the frame at the far right, the wooden sill running across below, and a strip of plain wall at the right edge. Beyond the glass only white winter sky.

COMPOSITION. Image 1 is an earlier take of this same painting, and it is right in camera, in the child (her head, hair, jacket, raised arm and pointing hand), the window, the sill, the frost fern, the light and the wash: paint the same picture again, everything the same size and in the same place, and change ONLY the woman's forearm and hand as described under ONE CORRECTION.

ONE CORRECTION to image 1. In image 1 the woman's forearm and hand are too heavy and too old: a thick arm, a broad thumb with wrinkled knuckles, creased skin. It must be the arm of the child's MOTHER, a slim, fit Chinese woman of thirty-five, exactly the hand and forearm of image 2 (its top right study is this very grip; its bottom right study is the colour): a slender wrist, a narrower forearm with smooth unlined skin, a long neat thumb with a short plain nail and no wrinkles, fine knuckles. Make the dishwater flush stronger: forearm and hand a clear pink-red, deepest at the knuckles, the thumb tip and the heel of the hand, still damp, with a few beads of water. The grip itself stays as in image 1: the thumb across the near side of the child's sleeve, the four fingers wrapped round the far side with their tips just showing past the sleeve's edge, the sleeve bunching a little under them. The arm still enters from the right edge at the same height and angle, with the rolled cream blouse sleeve at the edge.

WHAT IS HAPPENING. The mother has run up the stairs from the restaurant kitchen and reached her child in one movement. Her right hand has closed on the child's right forearm, over the white sleeve, just below the wrist: four fingers wrapped round the far side of the small arm, the thumb across the near side, the soft sleeve bunching under the grip. It is the grip of someone moving a child out of danger at once: firm, fast and sure, the knuckles a little raised, and not cruel: the fingers hold, they do not dig or twist. The pull has only just begun. The child's hand has not understood yet: her index finger is still extended and has only just left the glass, a finger's breadth of air between fingertip and pane, the other fingers loosely curled. The child's head has NOT turned: it still faces the window, tilted toward the frost, perfectly still.

THE TWO HANDS. The mother's: a Chinese woman of thirty-five, strong capable hands with short plain nails, no rings, no jewellery. Her bare forearm and hand are washed a clear pink-red from hot dishwater, distinctly pinker than ordinary skin, deepest at the knuckles and fingertips, and still damp. The plain cream cotton blouse sleeve is pushed up above her elbow; its rolled edge shows at the right edge of the picture. The child's: a FIVE-YEAR-OLD's hand, small, soft and plump, short fingers, tiny nails, a dimple over each knuckle; her whole forearm is thinner than the woman's wrist. Five fingers on each hand, carefully drawn.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. The sun is behind the glass, low, coming toward us. The glass, the sky and the frost are NOT PAINTED: bare cream paper left blank, carrying only the hair-thin lines of the frost. No yellow, no white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom, no clouds, no reflections: light is simply where the brush did not go. The wooden bar, the frame, the sill and the wall are in shade under one even transparent cool blue-grey ink wash with their ink lines showing through, no brown, no black. The child's head and shoulder, nearest to us, are under the same blue-grey wash: her hair is soft black ink wash with a gloss; her white jacket is line under the wash with its one orange band just visible round the upper arm. The raised sleeve against the bright glass is bare paper with thin outlines. Where wash meets bare paper, a clean, slightly soft wet edge.

THE ONE THING THE PICTURE IS ABOUT: the grip. The mother's pink hand and forearm closed on the small white sleeve, and the child's pointing hand above it, are the only things painted in full local colour and with the finest, most loving line in the picture, with a thin margin of clean bare paper round them. Everything else is line under wash.

THE FROST FERN on the pane, low at the right, partly hidden behind the woman's forearm: a real dendritic ice crystal, drawn with a single-hair brush in pale blue-grey line on bare paper, exactly as in image 1 and image 3: one straight main stem rising diagonally from the lower right corner of the pane toward the child's fingertip, straight side branches on both sides all at the same sixty-degree angle, each with smaller branches at the same angle, needle tips. Its tip is just below the child's fingertip.

THE WOOD. The frame, bar and sill were painted long ago and the paint is chipped and cracked: small flakes missing, hairline cracks, in the thinnest ink line under the blue-grey wash.
