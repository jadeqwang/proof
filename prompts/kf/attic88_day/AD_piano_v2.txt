A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. A five-year-old Chinese girl sits cross-legged on the bare hardwood floorboards of a dusty attic in the Netherlands, a winter morning in 1988, playing a paper keyboard that lies on the floor in front of her. We are very low, almost at floor level, close, on the far side of the paper from her, looking back across it and a little down. She fills the left and centre of the picture, bent well forward over her crossed legs, both arms reaching down and toward us to the paper; her bowed head is at the top of the picture, her face seen from the front and a little below, tipped down toward her hands. The paper keyboard lies in the lower right, running away from the bottom edge toward the right, its row of black keys along the edge nearest us. Behind her only the wide old floorboards running back into shade. Nothing else.

THE PRINTOUT. Two sheets of ordinary white printer paper, each landscape, joined end to end by one strip of clear, slightly shiny sticky tape across the join in the middle. Printed along it in slightly grey, slightly uneven toner: one row of piano keys seen flat, long white keys divided by thin lines, short black keys in groups of two and three. It is print on paper, with no thickness at all. Soft creases cross it at even intervals where it has been folded up like a road map, so each panel tilts a little; the corner nearest us curls up off the floor.

COMPOSITION. Image 1 is an earlier take of this same painting, and it is right in camera, in her pose, her face, her hair and clothes, the floor, the light and the wash: paint the same picture again, the same size and position of everything, and change ONLY the two things under TWO CORRECTIONS.

TWO CORRECTIONS to image 1.
One, the hands. In image 1 they are too big and too old: long bony fingers, an adult's hands. They must be the hands of a FIVE-YEAR-OLD, as in image 4 (an accepted painting of this same child's hands on this same paper keyboard, seen from above): small, soft and plump, about two thirds the size they are in image 1; short fingers with rounded tips and tiny short nails; a smooth padded back of the hand with a dimple over each knuckle; no tendons, no bony knuckles; a soft chubby wrist. All five digits of each hand visible and separate. Keep the same curved playing shape and the same places on the keys.
Two, the printout. Make it plainly what it is, as in image 4: two sheets of printer paper joined by ONE strip of clear, slightly shiny sticky tape that crosses the paper between her two hands; soft map-fold creases crossing it at even intervals so the panels tilt a little; the printed black keys in groups of two and three, in slightly grey toner; one corner curling up.

WHAT SHE IS DOING. She is playing a melody on the printed keys with both hands, and humming it quietly to herself with her mouth closed. Each hand has the trained shape of a small child who has had real piano lessons since before she was three: wrist low and level, knuckles raised into a round arch as if holding a small ball, all five fingers curved, fingertips on the paper, thumb on its side. In the hand nearer us one finger is lifted a centimetre, about to fall. Five fingers on each hand, short soft child's fingers, tiny nails. Her head is bowed and perfectly still. Her lips are together and level, closed on the hum, her jaw loose, her brows resting. She is not smiling and not frowning and not posing for anyone: all the life in the face is in the eyes, which are lowered and open, both irises pointed down at the fingertips of the hand nearer us, fixed and bright, the way a child looks when she is counting.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. Low winter sun comes from behind us and to the right and lies along the floor in a shallow layer no higher than her chest. It falls, as in image 1, on the paper keyboard, on both hands and both forearms, on her knees and shins, and on the strip of floorboards the paper lies on. Those sunlit parts are NOT PAINTED: bare cream paper left blank, with only thin ink outlines, the grey toner of the printed keys and the warm colour of her hands. No yellow, no white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom: light is simply where the brush did not go. Everything above the light, her face, hair, shoulders and upper arms, and all the floor behind her, is in shade: one even transparent cool blue-grey ink wash lies over it, with the ink lines showing through, no black. On her face the wash is a thin glaze laid OVER a normally painted face: her warm rosy flesh colour and the pink blush on her cheeks still show clearly through it, never grey, never ashen. Low in each eye there is one small crisp catch-light of bare paper: the bright sheet of paper below her, reflected. The line between shade and sun crosses her upper arms and chest as a clean, slightly soft wet edge.

THE ONE THING SHE IS ATTENDING TO: her hands on the paper keys. They are painted in full local colour and with the finest, most loving line in the picture: warm rosy flesh, pinker at the fingertips and knuckles, every printed key drawn, the faint shine of the tape, and a thin margin of completely clean bare paper round hands and printout so that they seem lit from within. Everything else stays line under wash.
