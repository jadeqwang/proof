A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. Very close on a small child's right hand at an old attic window, a winter morning in 1988: her index finger is on the cold glass, following a frost fern along its stem. We look from just above and behind her right shoulder, down and through the lower right pane of the window, so the wooden bars of the window run across the picture on a slant: the horizontal glazing bar crosses the top of the picture, the vertical glazing bar comes down through the left third, and they meet in the upper left: the cross of a four-pane window. Everything to the right of the vertical bar and below the horizontal one is ONE pane of glass, filling most of the picture; beyond the glass there is only white winter sky. In the lower left corner, close to us, the top of the child's head: glossy black hair only. From the bottom edge, a little left of centre, her right forearm in a white jacket sleeve with a ribbed cuff comes up into the picture, and her small hand, seen from the back, points up at the glass.

COMPOSITION. Follow image 1 exactly: the same camera, the same slant and positions of the two wooden bars, the same place and size of her sleeve, her hand and her extended index finger, and the same place, size, direction and branching of the frost fern, which runs from the lower right corner of the picture diagonally up to the centre and ends just beyond her fingertip. Image 1 is only a layout drawing from a 3D model: the ball, box and rod near the bottom are her cuff, her hand and her index finger, replace them with a real child's hand; the dark mass in the lower left corner is her hair; the other long slanting lines across the glass below the horizontal bar, and the two pointed shapes beside the vertical bar, are not objects, leave them out; and do not keep its mechanical even line.

WHAT SHE IS DOING. She has traced the frost fern with her fingertip all the way from its root, and has almost reached its tip. Her right index finger is straight and extended, the soft pad of the fingertip resting lightly on the glass exactly on the fern's main stem, a little short of the fern's last needle, which shows just beyond the fingertip. The other three fingers are curled loosely into the palm and the thumb rests against the side of the middle finger: a pointing hand, relaxed, careful, not pressing. It is the hand of a FIVE-YEAR-OLD: small, soft and plump, short fingers, a rounded fingertip with a tiny short nail, a dimple over each knuckle, no tendons, no veins. Five digits.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. The sun is behind the glass, low, coming toward us. The glass, the sky and the frost are NOT PAINTED: bare cream paper left blank, carrying only the hair-thin lines of the frost. No yellow, no white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom, no clouds, no reflections: light is simply where the brush did not go. The two wooden bars are in shade, under one even transparent cool blue-grey ink wash with their ink lines showing through, no brown, no black. Her hand and sleeve are in the sun that comes through the pane: the sleeve is bare paper with thin ink outlines and a breath of the blue-grey wash on its underside; her hair is soft black ink wash with a gloss. Where wash meets bare paper, a clean, slightly soft wet edge.

THE ONE THING SHE IS ATTENDING TO: the fingertip on the fern. These two things are drawn with the finest, most loving line in the picture. The hand is the only thing painted in full local colour: warm rosy flesh, the fingertip and the knuckles pinker from the cold glass. The frost fern is a real dendritic ice crystal, not a plant: one straight main stem; straight side branches leaving it on both sides, all at the same sixty-degree angle, so that the branches on each side are parallel; each side branch carrying its own smaller branches at that same angle, and those carrying tiny needles; every line running in one of only three directions; every branch tapering to a needle point; longest branches near the root, shortest at the tip. It is drawn with a single-hair brush in pale blue-grey line on bare paper, exactly as in image 3, where the same fern was younger and smaller; it has grown since. A thin margin of completely clean bare paper surrounds the fingertip and the crystal.

THE WOOD. The glazing bars were painted long ago and the paint is chipped and cracked: small irregular flakes missing, a fine network of hairline cracks that never quite repeats, a line of old dry putty where glass meets wood, in the thinnest ink line under the blue-grey wash.
