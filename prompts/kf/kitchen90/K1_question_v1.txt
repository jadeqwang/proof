A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. The eat-in kitchen of a small house in Decatur, Illinois, in 1990, in the evening after dinner. One pendant lamp with a cone-shaped enamelled metal shade hangs on its cord over a plain wooden kitchen table; it is the only light. A seven-year-old girl sits at the far side of the table, facing us, small, in the lamplight, her two hands on the table edge. Her parents stand behind her, one on each side, facing each other across the top of her head and talking fast in a dialect they believe she cannot follow. Her father stands at the left, turned toward the right; her mother stands at the right, turned toward the left. We sit across the table at the child's own eye height, so the table top runs across the bottom of the picture, the child's head is low in the frame between the two adults, and they rise above her to left and right. On the table in front of her: one blank sheet of paper and a pencil lying on it. Behind them, the kitchen's back wall: a double-hung sash window over the sink with the night outside, a chrome tap, a laminate counter, plain flat-doored wall cupboards to the left and right of the window; at the right a top-freezer refrigerator and an electric cooker with coil rings and an oven door; at the left and right edges of the picture, the ladder backs of two wooden kitchen chairs at the ends of the table.

WHAT THEY ARE DOING. THE MOTHER is in the middle of a word, asking a quick, exact question, low and fast: her eyes are on her husband's face, her brows slightly drawn in concentration, her mouth shaping a quick syllable, her chin a little forward. THE FATHER is considering it honestly before he answers: his head is slightly bowed, his lips together, his eyes aimed down and off to one side, at nothing, at the floor beyond the near corner of the table, and held there; his raised hand has stopped in the air like a conductor's just before the first beat. Neither of them looks at the child. THE CHILD is tapping: the fingers of her right hand are drumming on the table edge as on piano keys, one finger lifted, and her eyes are lowered to her own fingers. Her head is perfectly still. Her lips are together and level, her jaw loose, her brows resting. She is not smiling and not frowning and not posing for anyone. She looks as if she is somewhere else. She is listening to every word.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. The lamp throws one pool of light straight down. Image 2 is the lighting guide. The pool holds: the whole table top; the child, all of her, face, hair, sweatshirt, forearms and hands; and the two adults only from the chest down: their forearms and hands, the fronts of their bodies, the father's raised hand. Those lit parts are NOT PAINTED with any wash: bare cream paper left blank, with only thin ink outlines. No yellow, no white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom: light is simply where the brush did not go. The child's face, in the light, is fully and normally painted on that bare paper: thin line, her own warm rosy flesh wash, pink blush, dark eyes, glossy black ink hair. The shade of the lamp cuts the light off at the height of the adults' shoulders: their shoulders, necks and heads are above the light. Everything outside the pool is under one even transparent warm umber ink wash, a little deeper toward the ceiling and the corners: the two adults' heads and shoulders, the ceiling, the wall cupboards, the window (its night panes a deeper layer of the same umber, never black), the counter, sink and tap, the refrigerator and cooker, the outer chairs, the floor; with the ink lines showing through and no colour of their own. On the two adults' faces the wash is a thin glaze laid OVER normally painted faces: their warm flesh colour still shows clearly through it, never grey, never ashen; their eyes are clearly drawn, with one small round catch-light of bare paper in each. The edge between wash and blank paper is a clean, slightly soft wet edge; it crosses each adult at the chest.
