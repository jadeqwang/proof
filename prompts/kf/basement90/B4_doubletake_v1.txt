A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. The unfinished basement of a house in Decatur, Illinois, in 1990, at night. A father sits at his computer in a rolling office chair. A moment ago his seven-year-old daughter was standing at his elbow pointing at a mistake on the screen; he has fixed it and swung round in his chair to tell her so, and she is not there any more. We see him from the open floor beside the desk, from the height of a child, looking a little up at him. The desk: a plain wooden desk against a painted concrete-block wall, open underneath at its near end (two square legs) with a pedestal of drawers at its far end. On it: a flat beige horizontal desktop computer case with one floppy-disk slot, and on top of that a bulky beige 1990 CRT monitor, its screen a plain blue, the only light in the room; a beige keyboard on a coiled cable in front; a small closed box of floppy disks at the near end of the desk; a sheet of blank fan-fold printer paper standing behind the keyboard. The father fills the right half of the picture, seated, his chair turned toward us, the dark padded back of the chair behind him at the right. Overhead, exposed wooden floor joists at the upper right. And low at the left, in the dark of the knee space under the desk, small and easy to miss: his daughter, crouched on hands and feet like a cat, back rounded, looking out.

WHAT HE IS DOING. The double take. His body is still half toward the keyboard and his hands have not left it, but the chair has swivelled and his head has come right round toward us, toward the place at his elbow where she was standing. The words "Good catch!" are still in his mouth: mouth open on the last word, pleased, eyebrows shot up, eyes wide and bright behind the glasses. And his eyes have landed on empty air: both irises are aimed down and toward the left of the picture, at the height of a small child's face, where there is now nobody. The pleased look has not yet had time to change. He has not seen her under the desk. SHE, under the desk, is perfectly content and perfectly still, a cat: on hands and feet, back rounded, chin low, her face turned out toward the room, lips together and level, eyes bright.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. The screen is the only light and it faces out into the room, toward him. Image 2 shows where it falls (its lighter shapes): on his face, his throat and the front of his pale shirt, on his forearms and hands, on the keyboard and the front edge of the desk top, on his lap and knees, on the front of the chair back, and on a soft tall patch of the block wall at the far right. Those lit parts are NOT covered by any wash: bare cream paper left blank, with only thin ink outlines. No white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom: light is simply where the brush did not go. His face, in that light, is fully and normally painted as on his face sheet: thin line, his own warm flesh wash, dark irises; with the faintest transparent veil of pale screen blue on the cheek nearest the screen; in each eye one tiny upright blue-white rectangular catch-light, and in each lens of his glasses one small faint pale-blue rectangle, low, never hiding the eye. Everything else is under one even transparent deep indigo ink wash (clearly blue, an ink blue, not grey), laid in soft flat transparent layers: the wall behind the desk, the back and sides of the monitor and computer, the side of the desk, the floor, the joists, his back and the back of his head; and deepest of all under the desk, where the child is. She is found there only by thin line and a slightly paler glaze: her face is still a warm face under a deep glaze, never grey, with one tiny catch-light of bare paper in each eye. Soft, never flat black, never a solid fill; nothing under the wash has a colour of its own. Flat wash and line, very little modelling. The edge between wash and blank paper is a clean, slightly soft wet edge.

THE SCREEN is the one object with a colour of its own: one flat, even, transparent wash of clear medium blue, the blue of a DOS screen, completely plain: no text, no letters, no lines, no cursor, nothing on it.
