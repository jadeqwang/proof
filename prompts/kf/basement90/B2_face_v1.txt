A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. The unfinished basement of a house in Decatur, Illinois, in 1990, at night. A father sits at his computer, working; his seven-year-old daughter stands behind him on the base of his rolling office chair, holding on to his shoulders, and reads the screen over his shoulder. We are where the screen is, looking straight out at the two of them from the glass: two faces close together, lit by the screen, in a dark room. Father at the left of the picture, nearer to us and larger, seen from the chest up, the top of his hair cut by the top edge of the frame. Daughter just to the right of him and a little behind, her face in the middle of the picture, her chin about level with his shoulder. Behind them the basement falls away into the dark: exposed wooden floor joists overhead, one round steel support post, steel storage shelves with cardboard boxes at the far left, a painted concrete-block wall, the underside of an open wooden stair at the far right, one taped cardboard box on the floor. The computer, the screen and the desk are not in the picture: we are looking out from them.

WHAT THEY ARE DOING. Both of them are reading the screen, which is where we are, and neither is looking at us or at the other. HER: her head is perfectly still and faces us squarely, level. Her lips are together and level, her jaw loose, her brows resting. She is not smiling and not frowning and not posing for anyone: all the life in the face is in the eyes, which are fixed and bright on one exact word on the screen, the way a child looks when she is counting: both irises are turned a little toward the left side of the picture and slightly downward, converged on a point half a metre in front of her. HIM: the same face, grown. His head is level and faces us; his lips are together and level, his jaw loose, his brows resting; no smile, no frown; his eyes behind the glasses are fixed on a different line of the screen from hers, a little lower and toward the middle of the picture. He has no idea she is reading too. Two resting faces of one family side by side, doing the same thing with the same concentration: the same level mouth, the same quiet brows, the same steady eyes.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. The only light is the screen, and it shines from where we stand straight onto them. Image 2 shows it: the two faces, the front of his shirt, her fingers on his shoulders and the front of her sweatshirt are in the light; everything behind them is not. The lit parts are NOT covered by any wash: they are bare cream paper with thin ink outlines. No white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom: light is simply where the brush did not go. On that bare paper the two faces are fully and normally painted, as on their face sheets: thin line, their own warm rosy flesh wash, the pink blush on her cheeks, dark irises; and over the planes that face us (forehead, nose, cheekbones, chin) only the faintest transparent veil of pale screen blue. His shirt front and her fingers are bare paper and line. In each of her eyes and each of his there is one small crisp catch-light: a tiny upright rectangle with rounded corners, the screen itself, blue-white, in the same place in every eye. In each lens of his glasses one small faint pale-blue rectangle of reflection, low in the lens, never hiding the eye. Everything else is under one even transparent deep indigo ink wash, laid in soft transparent layers and deepening toward the back of the room: their hair, the sides of their heads and necks behind the ears, her shoulders, and the whole basement, with the ink lines of joists, post, shelves and stair showing through it and the paper grain breathing through it; soft, never flat black, never a solid fill; nothing under it has any colour of its own. The edge between the light on the faces and the wash is a clean, slightly soft wet edge.
