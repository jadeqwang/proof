A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. The unfinished basement of a house in Decatur, Illinois, in 1990, at night. A seven-year-old girl has hopped down from her father's chair and stands on the floor beside his desk, and with one small index finger she is touching the glass of his computer monitor, pointing at one exact place on the screen. We look over her shoulder from just behind and above her head, along her own line of sight. The monitor: a bulky beige 1990 CRT computer monitor, a deep plastic box with a thick rounded bezel, ventilation slots along its top, a small power button and two small knobs under the screen, standing on top of a flat beige horizontal desktop computer case with one floppy-disk slot. Its screen is the only light in the room: a plain, even blue, with nothing on it at all. In front of us, from behind: the back of the girl's head, large, low in the middle of the frame, glossy black hair with a small half-ponytail tied high at the crown; and at the right edge the back and side of her father's head and one shoulder, close and soft, as he sits facing the screen. On the desk at the lower left a small closed box of floppy disks; behind the monitor at the right a stack of blank fan-fold printer paper leaning against the painted concrete-block wall; the wooden desk top.

WHAT SHE IS DOING. She is pointing at the one word he missed. Her right arm, the grey sleeve pushed up to the elbow, reaches forward away from us; we see the hand from behind and a little above: the back of the hand, the knuckles, the index finger stretched out straight, the other three fingers curled into the palm, the thumb tucked along the side. The pad of the index fingertip is pressed flat against the glass, at a point left of the middle of the screen and a little above its middle; the fingertip is slightly squashed by the pressure, and the glass holds a small soft reflection of it. Five fingers, small and correct. Her head is still and tipped a little forward toward the finger. Her father has not turned yet: his head still faces the screen.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. The screen is the only light. Image 2 shows it: the screen itself, and what faces it. The light falls on her pointing hand and forearm, on the curve of her cheek and the edge of her sleeve as seen from behind, on the edge of her father's cheek, ear and shoulder, and on the desk top and the top of the computer case directly in front of the screen. Those lit parts are NOT PAINTED: bare cream paper left blank, with only thin ink outlines. No white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom: light is simply where the brush did not go. Everything else is under one even transparent deep indigo ink wash, laid in soft transparent layers: the beige casing of the monitor and the computer (line under wash, no beige), the wall, the paper stack, the box of disks, the backs of both heads and her father's shoulder; soft, a little deeper in the corners, with the ink lines showing through it and the paper grain breathing through it; never flat black, never a solid fill. Hair stays glossy black ink. The edge between wash and blank paper is a clean, slightly soft wet edge.

THE ONE THING SHE IS ATTENDING TO: the screen under her fingertip. The screen is the only object in the picture with a colour of its own: one flat, even, transparent wash of clear medium blue, the blue of a DOS screen, a breath paler toward the middle, the glass gently bulging with softly rounded corners. It is completely plain: no text, no letters, no lines, no cursor, no window, no icons, no reflections of the room. Against it, the centre of interest of the whole picture is her fingertip on the glass: the pointing hand is painted with the finest, most loving line in the picture and in warm rosy flesh colour, each knuckle and the small nail drawn, with a thin margin of clean bare paper round the hand and finger where the screen light wraps round them, so that the hand seems lit from within.
