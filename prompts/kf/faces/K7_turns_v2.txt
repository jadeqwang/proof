SUBJECT. A kitchen in Decatur, Illinois, 1990, a bright morning, seen wide from beside the kitchen table. In the middle of the picture a seven-year-old Chinese girl stands alone on the linoleum floor in a shaft of sun, turned toward the left of the picture, where the light comes from. At the right her mother has turned her back and is walking away toward the hall doorway. The room: at the far left the corner of a white enamel stove and above it an upper cupboard; next to it a tall refrigerator with a freezer door on top and a long upright handle; in the left foreground an empty wooden kitchen chair with two back rails, pulled out and facing the girl, and the corner of the kitchen table cutting across the bottom left corner; behind the two figures a tall china cabinet with two glazed doors and three shelves of plain bowls and cups; at the far right the upright frame of the hall doorway.

WHAT THE GIRL IS DOING. A moment ago her mother's thumbs were holding her cheeks up in a smile. The instant her mother's back was turned she let it go. Her shoulders are on their way down, her arms hang loose at her sides, her weight has settled on both bare feet. Her head is perfectly still. Her lips are together and level, her jaw loose, her brows resting. She is not smiling and not frowning and not posing for anyone: all the life in the face is in the eyes, which are fixed and bright on one tiny thing floating in the sunlight an arm's length in front of her face, a little above her eye level, toward the left of the picture, the way a child looks when she is counting. Both irises are turned to that point. Do not draw the thing she is watching. Nothing of the smile is left.

She is seen from behind in back three-quarter view, in mid-step toward the doorway at the right, upright and quick, her arms at her sides, her head turned away from us toward the doorway so that we see only her permed black curls, one ear, the wire arm of her glasses over it and the line of her cheek and jaw: none of her features. LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. One shaft of morning sun comes from a window out of frame at the left and slants down across the room from upper left to lower right; image 2 and image 6 show where it falls (in image 6 white is sunlit). In the painting the shaft is a little higher than in those guides at the girl, so that it takes in ALL of her: her face, her head, her sweatshirt, her legs and feet, and with her the seat and back rails of the empty chair and a slanted patch of floor around her feet; of the mother it reaches only the skirt and legs below the hip. Everything in the sun is NOT PAINTED: bare cream paper left blank, with only thin ink outlines. No yellow, no white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom: light is simply where the brush did not go. On the girl's sunlit face the skin is bare paper, the features in the finest ink line of the picture, a warm pink blush on the cheek, a dark iris with one small crisp catch-light of bare paper; her hair is glossy black ink. Everything else, the mother's back, head and shoulders, the china cabinet and its dishes, the stove, the refrigerator, the table, the walls and the doorway, is in shade: one even transparent warm umber ink wash, the ink lines showing through it, nothing in it darker than a mid brown, no black, the things in it drawn in line under the wash with no colour of their own. The edge of the shaft across the floor, the cabinet and the mother's skirt is a clean, slightly soft wet edge. The girl is the only thing in the room the light is on completely.
