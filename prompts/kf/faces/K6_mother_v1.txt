WHAT SHE IS DOING. She is getting her daughter's face right. With both hands (out of frame) she is setting the child's cheeks, and she is watching the result the way a swimmer on the blocks watches the water. Her head is perfectly still, tipped forward a very little. Her eyes look just below the lens, both irises lowered a fraction and converged on the child's mouth, steady and exact. Her lips are together and level, her jaw loose, her brows resting. She is not smiling and not frowning and not posing for anyone: all the life in the face is in the eyes. It is the same kind of face her daughter has, and she has no idea that her own is doing it.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. Image 2 shows it: the daylight is BEHIND her. The window panes at the left, the sill and the strip of counter and sink under the window are NOT PAINTED: bare cream paper left blank, with only thin ink outlines. No yellow, no white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom: light is simply where the brush did not go. (the glazing bars and the tap are thin ink lines on the blank paper; nothing is seen outside the window). Where the sun passes over her from behind, it leaves only the top surface of her two shoulders as bare paper. Everything else, her face, her hair, the stove, the cupboard, the refrigerator and the wall, is in shade: one even transparent warm umber ink wash, the ink lines showing through it, nothing in it darker than a mid brown, no black. On her face the wash is a thin glaze laid OVER a normally painted face: her warm rosy flesh colour and a little warm blush on the cheeks still show clearly through it, never grey, never ashen. Her face is drawn with as much care and as fine a line as any face in the film. In each eye, low in the iris, one small crisp catch-light of bare paper: the reflection of her daughter's sunlit face in front of her. The boundary between the bare paper and the wash is a clean, slightly soft wet edge.
