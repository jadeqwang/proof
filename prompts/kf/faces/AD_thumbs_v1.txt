A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. A five-year-old Chinese girl sits on the floor of a dusty attic above a restaurant in the Netherlands on a winter morning in 1988, facing us, seen level with her eyes from very close: her head and shoulders fill the middle of the frame. Two adult hands come into the picture, one from the left edge and one from the right edge, toward her cheeks. They belong to her mother, who is where we are: only her two hands, wrists and forearms are in the picture. Behind the child: the sloping underside of the roof with exposed rafters at the upper left, a plain plastered wall, an upright timber post at the upper right, old floorboards.

COMPOSITION. Follow image 1: the same camera, the child's head in the middle of the frame at the same size, her eyes two fifths of the way down, her shoulders leaving the frame at the bottom, the rafters and the post where they are. Image 1 is only a layout drawing from a 3D model: replace its mannequin with the real child (the slab across the top of the head is her fringe), and do not keep its mechanical even line. The two large shapes coming in from the left and right edges of image 1 are the mother's forearms, and the blocks at their ends are her hands: paint them as real forearms and hands, smaller than those blocks, and do NOT put them beside her ears: each hand stops in the air beside a cheek, a hand's width short of touching it, level with her mouth.

THE HANDS. Both hands are reaching for her cheeks, thumbs leading: each hand is open and relaxed, palm turned toward the child's face, the thumb stretched out in front and pointing at the cheek beside the corner of her mouth, the four fingers loosely curved behind it. The left hand comes from the left edge and the right hand from the right edge, mirror images of each other, like the two hands in the top-left study of image 6. They have not touched her: there is clear air, one hand's width, between each thumb tip and her cheek. Her hands: strong capable swimmer's hands with short plain nails, no rings, no jewellery; five fingers on each, carefully drawn. The sleeves of a plain cream cotton blouse are pushed up above the elbows, so the forearms are bare; forearms and hands are flushed pink from hot dishwater, pinker than the child's skin, and that pink still shows through the shade.

WHAT THE GIRL IS DOING. A grown-up has just told her that her face has no expression and that she should try to have some, right now. So she makes one, on purpose, with great care. ONE eye, the eye on the LEFT of the picture, is narrowed to a slit: the lower lid pushed up and the upper lid lowered until only a thin dark line of the iris still shows between the lashes. It is a squint, not a wink: the eye is not shut. The other eye stays wide open. Both brows are drawn down and together, the way she has seen grown-ups do when they work out a hard sum, with one small crease between them; no wrinkles on the nose. Her lips are pressed together in a level line, the corners neither up nor down, and she lets out a small sigh through her nose: the chest and shoulders just sinking. It is a five-year-old's honest idea of what having an expression is: a little too much, completely sincere, studious rather than cross. She is not angry and she is not sulking; she is trying. It is funny because she means it. She is still holding that face. Her head has not moved and still faces us squarely, but her open eye has slid sideways: both irises are turned toward the hand at the RIGHT side of the picture, watching it come.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. In this closer frame there is no sunlight at all (image 2: everything is shade): the sun is on her lap, below the bottom edge. So nothing is bare paper except the catch-lights: her face, hair, shoulders and the whole room are in shade: one even transparent cool blue-grey ink wash lies over it all, a little darker under the roof. On her face the wash is a thin glaze laid OVER a normally painted face: her warm rosy flesh colour and the pink blush on her cheeks still show clearly through it, never grey, never ashen. In each eye there is one small crisp catch-light of bare paper, squarish like a distant window, on the same side in both eyes. The mother's hands and forearms are under the same wash, warm and pink underneath it. No black anywhere.
