A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. A kitchen in Decatur, Illinois, 1990, a bright morning. A seven-year-old Chinese girl stands facing us, very close, head and shoulders, seen level with her eyes from where her seated mother's face is. Her mother's two hands come up into the picture from below and hold the child's face, pushing her cheeks up into the shape of a smile. Of the mother only the two hands, the wrists and the forearms in cardigan sleeves are in the picture. Behind the child: a plain kitchen wall, and at the far left edge the upright line of a door frame. Nothing else.

COMPOSITION. Follow image 1 exactly: the same camera, the child's head in the upper middle of the frame at the same size, her eyes two fifths of the way down, her shoulders leaving the frame at the bottom, the two adult forearms rising from the bottom edge left and right of centre and converging on her face. Image 1 is only a layout drawing from a 3D model: replace its mannequin with the real child (the slab across the top of the head is her fringe) and its mitts with real hands, and do not keep its mechanical even line. CORRECT ONE THING in image 1: there the hands meet under her chin; in the painting they are higher, ON HER CHEEKS, as in the top-middle study of image 4.

REPAINT. Image 5 is an earlier take of this exact painting and it is nearly right: repaint it as it is, the same child, the same hands with the thumbs on her cheeks, the same pushed-up mouth, the same light and wash, the same drawing hand. Change only her brows and the set of her head. In image 5 the inner ends of her brows are raised, which reads as worry. In the painting they do the opposite: both brows are pulled DOWN hard and together, low over the eyes, their inner ends lower than their outer ends, with a tight knot of two short creases between them: a scowl of pure effort, the top half of the face refusing what the bottom half is being made to do. And her head pulls a little further back and away toward the right of the picture, against the hands, chin tucked. Her eyes stay slid to the right corners, looking for the way out. She is not sad, not hurt and not afraid: she is a small cross person being smiled by force, and it is funny.

THE HANDS. Each of the mother's hands cups one side of the child's jaw, the four fingers lying along the jaw and under the ear, and the pad of each thumb is pressed on the soft cheek just beside a corner of the mouth, pushing up and a little outward. The two thumbs do not touch each other and are not under the chin: there is the whole width of the mouth between them. Under the thumbs the cheeks bunch up into two round cushions below the eyes and the closed mouth is dragged into a wide upturned curve: a smile made by someone else's thumbs. The hands are careful and fond, the way a mother straightens a collar; they are not gripping. Her hands: strong capable swimmer's hands with short plain nails, no rings, no jewellery; five fingers on each, carefully drawn. The cuffs of a soft knitted cardigan at her wrists.

WHAT THE GIRL IS DOING. She is trying to get out of it. Above the pushed-up smile her brows come down hard and together, with a small knot between them, deeper than a moment ago; her eyes have slid as far as they go toward the right side of the picture, both irises in the right corners of the eyes, looking for the way out; her head is pulling back and turning a little away from the hands toward that side, chin tucked, shoulders coming up, like a child squirming away from a face cloth. It is funny and it is tender: nobody is being hurt, and nobody is afraid. The top half of her face and the bottom half do not agree.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. One shaft of morning sun from a window behind us falls full on her: her whole face, her neck, the front of her sweatshirt and both of the mother's hands and wrists are in it (image 2 shows the shaft reaching only to her chin: in the painting it reaches higher, to the top of her fringe). Everything in the sun is NOT PAINTED: bare cream paper left blank, with only thin ink outlines. No yellow, no white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom: light is simply where the brush did not go. On her sunlit face the skin is bare paper: the features are drawn in the finest ink line of the whole picture, with only a wide warm pink blush on the bunched cheeks, a soft rose on the lips, and dark irises each holding one small crisp catch-light of bare paper, upright and oblong like a window. Her hair is glossy black ink. The upper edge of the shaft crosses her hair just above the fringe, slanting gently down to the right, as a clean, slightly soft wet edge; the crown of her head above it, the wall behind her and the door frame are in shade: one even transparent warm umber ink wash, the ink lines showing through it, nothing in it darker than a mid brown, no black. The mother's forearms pass from shade at the bottom edge of the frame into the sun at the wrists.

THE GIRL. Little Jade at about age 7, Decatur, Illinois, 1990: a Chinese girl in first or second grade, with LARGE round dark eyes set wide apart, each with thick dark upper lashes; distinct, fairly strong, straight dark eyebrows; a tiny button nose; a small soft mouth; soft cheeks with a wide warm pink blush. Proportions of a seven-year-old: about five and a half heads tall, longer and thinner arms and legs than a small child, a round face with the chin a touch longer. Glossy jet-black hair (pure black ink, never brown): a light fringe of a few soft parted strands with the forehead showing between them, long side locks in front of the ears, a small half-ponytail tied high at the back of the crown with a dark band, the rest straight down to her shoulder blades. An oversized plain heather-grey crew-neck sweatshirt with the sleeves pushed up to the elbows, no print and no lettering; dark navy stirrup leggings; bare feet.
