SUBJECT. Shanghai, a summer afternoon in 1986. We look steeply down through the eyes of a three-year-old girl who sits cross-legged on bare floorboards, at the patch of floor directly in front of her. We do not see her head or face. On the clear patch of sunlit boards in the middle of the frame she has laid out EIGHT small pieces of torn white toilet tissue. They are the eight eighths of one square sheet she has torn in half, and in half again, and in half again, and she has laid them back down where they came from, so that together they still make the square of the one sheet: two rows of four small upright rectangles. The arrangement shows the three tears: the gap straight down the middle, between the second and third columns, is the widest (the first tear, one sheet into two); the gap between the upper row and the lower row is narrower (the second tear, two into four); and the gap inside each pair, between columns one and two and between columns three and four, is the narrowest, the two pieces almost touching (the third tear, four into eight). So the eight pieces read at a glance as FOUR PAIRS: within each pair the two pieces lie so close that only a hairline of floor shows between them; between the left pair and the right pair of each row there is a clear gap as wide as a child's finger; between the upper row and the lower row a gap about half that. Left half and right half of the arrangement are plainly two groups. Exactly eight pieces, no more in the middle. Round the edges of the frame: her two bare chubby knees and shins at the left and right edges, the hem of her pale gingham smock dress, and the near edge of the drift of torn tissue she is sitting in: larger torn scraps overlapping at the lower left and lower right, a few small scraps on the boards at the top.
