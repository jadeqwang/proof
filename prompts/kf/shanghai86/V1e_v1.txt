A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. One upstairs room of a lilong house in Shanghai, a summer afternoon in 1986, seen from low down near the floor. In the left foreground a three-year-old Chinese girl sits cross-legged on bare floorboards in a pool of window light, seen from her right side in three-quarter view, bowed over the floor in front of her, in a drift of torn white toilet tissue: soft heaps of it round her legs and scraps scattered across the lit boards. Behind her, the corner of a wooden double bed with a straw mat and folded quilts, and two tall bed posts carrying a mosquito-net frame. In the right wall, right of centre, an open doorway: a plain wooden door frame, the door leaf swung away outward, a dim landing beyond. Her mother stands in the doorway, full figure, small in the frame, having just come in. At the far right a tall dark wooden wardrobe with a mirror door. Plain plastered walls.

COMPOSITION. Follow image 1 exactly: the same camera, the same perspective, the same positions and sizes of the child, the bed and its posts, the doorway, the mother, the wardrobe and the drift on the floor. Image 1 is only a layout drawing from a 3D model: replace its mannequins with the real people and its boxes with the real things, and do not keep its mechanical even line. In image 1 the stacked boxes on the bed are folded quilts; the big panel at the right is the wardrobe with its mirror door; the flat ovals and small rectangles on the floor are the heaps and scraps of torn tissue: paint them as real torn tissue, irregular and soft, none a neat rectangle; the small boxes at the mother's feet are her black cloth shoes.

WHAT THEY ARE DOING. The child has not noticed anyone. She is still ripping: both small hands are close together low in front of her, and between the thumbs and forefingers of both hands she is tearing one tiny scrap of tissue smaller still. Her head is bowed and perfectly still, both eyes lowered onto the scrap in her fingers; her lips are together and level, her jaw loose, her brows resting; she is not smiling: all the life in the face is in the eyes, fixed on that one scrap, the way a child looks when she is counting. The mother has stopped dead on the threshold in the first half-second of seeing this: her right hand has just caught the door frame at shoulder height, her weight is still on her front foot, her left arm hangs. Her head is tipped slightly forward and both her eyes are fixed on the child and the paper; her eyebrows are just beginning to rise and her lips have just parted to speak. She has not yet decided whether to be cross or to laugh.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. Afternoon sun comes through a window out of frame behind us at the left and falls as a pool on the floorboards in the left foreground. Image 2 shows exactly where it falls (its white shapes: the pool on the floor, the tissue drift, the child's arms, lap and front) and where it does not (its greys). The pool points toward the door but stops on the floor well short of the mother's feet. The sunlit floor and the tissue are NOT PAINTED: bare cream paper left blank, with only thin ink outlines. No yellow, no white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom: light is simply where the brush did not go. Everything the sun does not touch, the walls, the bed, the doorway, the wardrobe, the far floor and the whole of the mother, is under one even transparent warm grey-green ink wash, a little darker in the doorway and under the bed, with the ink lines showing through it and no black anywhere. The edge between shade and sun on the floor is a clean, slightly soft wet edge. The child is in the light: her skin is painted in its normal pale warm rosy flesh wash with pink blush, no grey over it. The mother is in shade: on her face the wash is a thin glaze laid OVER a normally painted face: her warm rosy flesh colour still shows clearly through it, never grey, never ashen, and in each eye, behind her glasses, one small crisp catch-light of bare paper, squarish like a window. The faded red of her gingham dress is the one faint colour allowed outside the light, and it stays pale.

THE ONE THING THE CHILD IS ATTENDING TO: the torn tissue. It is the whitest and most carefully drawn thing in the picture: bare cream paper with the finest pale grey line, every torn edge feathered, hair-thin fibres standing out from the scraps, the heaps soft and deep like fresh snow, each scrap with a hair-thin pale warm shadow so that it reads against the sunlit boards. Plain rough 1980s Chinese toilet tissue in loose sheets: no print, no perforations, no roll.

Toddler proportions: a very big round head, about four heads tall, a short neck, short chubby arms and legs, small dimpled hands, a round tummy. Her face is round and short: full baby cheeks, a short chin, a small nose. Clothes (1986 Shanghai, home-made, plain): a loose short-sleeved cotton smock dress to the knee in a faded pale red-and-white small gingham check, with a plain white round collar; bare arms and legs; black cotton cloth shoes with one strap, no socks. She is 3, not 5 and not a baby: she stands and sits steadily. Image 3 is the child's model sheet (body, dress, hair, face in side and three-quarter view).
