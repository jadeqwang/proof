A painting on cream xuan paper, wide 16:9, in one hand: fine-line baimiao ink outlines and transparent ink wash, the paper grain and a few long paper fibres showing through everywhere.

SUBJECT. One upstairs room of a lilong house in Shanghai, a summer afternoon in 1986. A three-year-old Chinese girl sits cross-legged on bare floorboards in the middle of the room, facing us, centred, seen level with her eyes from about two metres. All round her on the floor lies a drift of torn toilet tissue, heaped like snow: soft mounds of shredded rough white tissue paper close round her knees, and loose scraps, strips and crumbs of it scattered outward across the boards to both edges of the frame. Behind her, along the back wall, a wooden double bed seen side-on: its long side rail, a straw mat on the mattress, folded cotton quilts and two pillows at the right, and at its left corner a tall bed post with a white mosquito net tied back to it. At the far left a dark wooden chest of drawers with five drawers. A plain plastered wall. Nothing else.

WHAT SHE IS DOING. She is still ripping. Her two small hands are close together in front of her tummy, and between the thumbs and forefingers of BOTH hands she holds one tiny scrap of tissue, no bigger than a postage stamp, and is tearing it in two, smaller still: a small rip is opening between her two thumbs, with a few torn fibres stretching across it. Each hand has five small dimpled fingers; the other fingers are loosely curled. Her head is bowed a little and perfectly still; both eyes are lowered onto the scrap between her fingers, so we see her upper eyelids and thick lashes and the dark irises under them pointing down at that exact spot. Her lips are together and level, her jaw loose, her brows resting. She is not smiling and not frowning and not posing for anyone: all the life in the face is in the eyes, fixed and bright on the tiny tear, the way a child looks when she is counting. Bits of tissue cling to her dress, her knees and her hair.

LIGHT, the most important thing in the picture. Afternoon sun comes through a window out of frame at the left and falls as a pool of light on the floorboards in the middle of the room; the child sits wholly inside it. Image 2 shows where the sunlight falls (its white and palest shapes: the pool on the floor, the drift, her arms, hands, lap and the front of her) and where it does not (its greys: the wall, the bed, the chest of drawers, the floor beyond the pool). The sunlit floor and the tissue are NOT PAINTED: bare cream paper left blank, with only thin ink outlines. No yellow, no white paint, no glow, no rays, no haze, no bloom: light is simply where the brush did not go. Everything the sun does not touch is under one even transparent warm grey-green ink wash, a little darker under the bed and in the corners, with the room's ink lines showing through it and no black anywhere. The edge between shade and sun on the floor is a clean, slightly soft wet edge. The child is in the light: her face, arms and legs are painted in their normal pale warm rosy flesh wash with the pink blush on her cheeks and no grey over them, brightest on the side toward the window (the left of the picture), with only the faintest warm shadow under the fringe and chin. In each eye one small crisp catch-light of bare paper, squarish like a window. The faded red of her gingham dress is the one faint colour allowed outside the light, and it stays pale.

THE ONE THING SHE IS ATTENDING TO: the torn tissue. It is the whitest, softest and most carefully drawn thing in the picture: bare cream paper with the finest pale grey line, every torn edge feathered, every scrap with its own ragged outline and a few hair-thin fibres standing out from it, the heaps soft and deep like fresh snow, each scrap casting only a hair-thin pale warm shadow on the boards so that it reads against the sunlit floor. The lit floorboards carry their board joints, grain lines and nail heads in thin line; the tissue carries nothing but its own fibres. No pattern, no print, no perforations on the tissue: it is rough plain 1980s Chinese toilet tissue in loose square sheets, not a roll.
